# Thumbnail art direction

Read when selecting a visual concept, adapting to a niche, or making variants. Treat these as choices, not templates to apply mechanically.

## Choose a visual story

| Story | Best fit | Composition | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Result first | Builds, makeovers, demonstrations | Large finished result with one detail revealing why it matters | Claiming a result absent from the video |
| Visible tension | Challenges, investigations, experiments | Subject confronting a concrete obstacle or contradiction | Vague reaction face with no visible cause |
| Comparison | Reviews, tests, transformations | Two clearly distinguishable subjects, matched scale, one comparison axis | Too many labels or an invented before/after |
| One revealing detail | Tutorials, analysis, discoveries | Close crop on the important mechanism or detail, minimal context | Tiny screenshot that requires reading |
| Human stakes | Personal stories, interviews, journeys | Authentic expression plus one contextual object or setting | Generic astonishment unrelated to the story |
| Scale or isolation | Travel, engineering, large projects | Strong size relationship or lone subject in a readable setting | Wide scenic shot with no focal point |

Without a provided face, use the actual object, scene, process, or result when it can carry the hook. A person is not mandatory.

## Fit the audience

- **Software/tutorials:** Magnify the relevant interface detail or outcome. Avoid full-window screenshots with unreadable menus. Preserve exact UI when supplied; do not fabricate evidence of a feature working.
- **Product reviews:** Make the real product recognizable. Use material, angle, condition, or a supported comparison to create interest rather than surrounding it with invented specifications.
- **Education/documentary:** Show the concrete question, mechanism, location, or artifact. Historical or scientific claims must come from the brief or verified sources, not decorative text.
- **Gaming:** Use supplied gameplay or recognizable subject references. Choose a moment with readable action; avoid unrelated HUD fragments and extra tiny characters.
- **Lifestyle/travel:** Let place, light, texture, or the actual transformation establish appeal. Preserve authenticity of supplied people and settings.

## Title and thumbnail work together

Use the supplied title to establish what the viewer already knows. The image should add the missing visual information.

Example: for "I built a desk in a tiny closet," show the actual compact workspace and its spatial constraint. Short text such as "IT FITS" may help if the video supports that result. Repeating the full title over a generic desk adds little.

Example: for "Why my 3D prints kept failing," show the relevant failure beside the implicated detail, using supplied evidence. Do not invent a specific cause or headline such as "ONE SETTING" unless the video supports it.

## Variants with a purpose

First distinguish controlled tests from creative exploration using the user's wording; ordinary "A/B variants" means a controlled test.

- **Controlled test:** Keep title, subject, crop, lighting, and palette stable while changing one hypothesis, such as headline versus no headline or one headline versus another. State the intended difference and inspect for unintended generator drift. Disclose meaningful drift instead of claiming only one variable changed.
- **Exploration:** Try distinct visual stories, such as result first versus visible tension. Label the concept rather than pretending it isolates a single factor.
- **Existing brand:** Reuse supplied type hierarchy, palette, crops, and recurring motifs. Improve the specific thumbnail without resetting the channel's identity.

Judge variants on clarity and fidelity before delivery. Actual performance requires real audience data; a design assessment is not a CTR prediction.
